A Materialist Theory of Anarchism
Working notes toward a materialist account of decentralized freedom, technological abundance, and human flourishing

The documents should not be mistaken for independently verified academic scholarship or assumed to be wholly human-authored prose.

1. Purpose
This document investigates the possibility of developing a contemporary materialist theory of anarchism.

The starting inspiration is Karl Marx: his analysis of capitalism, historical development, productive forces, class relations, and the contradictions between technological capabilities and existing economic structures.

The objective is to investigate whether a comparable analytical framework could be developed around anarchist principles, particularly in light of contemporary technological developments.

The central question:

What happens when humanity develops the productive capabilities to provide greater freedom and material security, but its economic and political institutions continue to restrict access to those capabilities?

This is not an attempt to establish a finished ideology. It is an evolving investigation into existing scholarship, historical experience, contemporary technology, and possible forms of social organization. The project should preserve disagreements, competing theories, and unresolved questions rather than prematurely forcing everything into one ideological framework.

The originating problem

The project began with a practical concern about creative work: people may want to release, share, or show what they make, but hold back because they fear that others could copy it, reproduce it, redistribute it, or profit from it without their permission. That fear is often connected to material survival. When a creator depends on sales, commissions, licensing, or the exclusivity of a work to pay for necessities, making the work freely available can put their livelihood at risk.

This is not simply a question of whether creators are generous or whether sharing is good. Under existing economic conditions, protecting control over a work may be part of protecting the means by which someone lives. A creator can value collaboration and openness while still having good reason to hesitate when the financial consequences of unauthorized use fall primarily on them.

The initial question was whether AI-assisted production and increasingly accessible creative tools might change what people can make, reproduce, and build upon—and whether different material and social arrangements could make sharing and creative freedom less likely to conflict with economic security. The project does not assume that creators should release everything, that copying is always harmless, or that attribution and control no longer matter. It investigates what would allow people to choose whether, how, and under what terms to share their work without making that choice a threat to survival.

How to read this document. Sections 3 through 8 review intellectual foundations and existing research. Sections 9 through 13 develop the project's provisional argument and research questions. Sections 14 through 16 collect references and record research status. Sections 17 through 27 broaden the inquiry toward human flourishing and civilizational futures. Section 28 begins a case-study approach to concrete technologies that may expand human capabilities. These divisions are guides, not sealed categories.

What this document is committed to. Do not assume that technological progress automatically produces freedom. Investigate who controls it, who benefits from it, what institutions shape it, and what alternative arrangements are materially possible. The ultimate objective is to understand how human productive capabilities can support a society in which people are increasingly free to organize their lives, cooperate, create, and flourish without unnecessary economic or political domination.

2. Initial thesis
Human productive capabilities are historically developed through accumulated knowledge, cooperation, science, technology, and social organization.

However, the institutions governing access to those capabilities do not necessarily develop at the same rate.

Capitalism has historically played a major role in developing productive capacity, industrial infrastructure, scientific research, and technological innovation. At the same time, private ownership, competition, profit requirements, and economic dependency shape how those capabilities are distributed and used.

As production becomes increasingly automated, decentralized, and accessible, contradictions may emerge between what people can collectively produce and the economic arrangements governing their ability to benefit from it.

The possibility worth investigating is whether these developments create conditions for increasingly decentralized, cooperative, and non-hierarchical forms of organization.

The objective would not simply be to abolish existing institutions, but to understand what social arrangements could replace their useful functions without reproducing unnecessary domination.

3. Intellectual foundations
3.1 Adam Smith, Marx, Hegel, and historical materialism

Adam Smith (1723–1790)

Smith is relevant as a major figure in the development of classical political economy and as a moral philosopher whose work examines the relationship between individual judgment, social cooperation, commercial exchange, and public institutions. He should not be reduced to a slogan about self-interest or treated as a straightforward advocate of every institution later associated with capitalism. Smith wrote before the modern term "capitalism" became established; his central subject was commercial society.

Important concepts:

- Sympathy and the impartial spectator as elements of moral judgment.
- The division of labour and its effects on productivity, skill, and social organization.
- Exchange, markets, prices, wages, profits, rent, and the distribution of the social product.
- Commercial society, natural liberty, and the role of institutions in coordinating economic activity.
- The danger of monopolies, collusion, and merchants using political influence to advance their own interests.
- Public works, public institutions, and education as matters that cannot always be left to private profitability.

Important works:

- The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759).
- An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776).
- Lectures on Jurisprudence (student notes from Smith's lectures, published posthumously).

Why Smith matters to this project: Smith offers an influential account of how productive capacity and social coordination develop through specialization and exchange. His work also makes clear that political economy cannot be separated cleanly from moral philosophy: questions of prosperity, justice, public institutions, and the treatment of ordinary people belong in the same investigation. This makes him useful for examining the historical arguments for commercial society before comparing markets with commons, mutualism, cooperatives, and peer production.

Smith is also important because his account contains tensions relevant to the project's questions. The division of labour can greatly increase output, but Smith warns that repetitive, narrowly specialized work can diminish workers' intellectual and social capacities unless education and public provision counteract those effects. He criticizes systems of privilege and the ability of organized commercial interests to shape public policy, while still defending markets and a limited role for government. His work therefore offers neither a simple endorsement nor a simple rejection of market society.

What this project takes from Smith: the need to understand how specialization, exchange, institutions, and unintended consequences shape productive capacity; the connection between economic arrangements and moral/social life; and the importance of examining how commercial interests can influence political rules.

What this project leaves open: whether market coordination can be combined with more democratic or decentralized ownership; how far Smith's account of natural liberty can address structural inequalities and concentrated private power; and which of his historical observations remain applicable under modern industrial, digital, and AI-mediated production. Smith should not be retroactively classified as an anarchist or assumed to have anticipated contemporary alternatives.

Karl Marx (1818–1883)

Important concepts:

Historical materialism.

Productive forces and relations of production.

Class relations.

Alienation.

Surplus value.

Capital accumulation.

Historical contradictions.

The relationship between economic structures and political institutions.

Marx analyzed capitalism as a historically specific mode of production, examining how it transformed productive capacity and social relations while also criticizing its structures of class domination and exploitation. His analysis treats capitalism as a system that emerged under particular historical conditions, develops through internal tensions, and is therefore neither permanent nor inevitable.

What this project takes from Marx: the method of analyzing capitalism as a historically developing system; the distinction between productive forces and relations of production; the insistence that economic structures shape political institutions; and the idea that contradictions between developed capabilities and existing social relations create pressure for change.

What this project leaves open: whether Marx's account of class, the state, and historical inevitability is compatible with anarchist commitments; whether the labor theory of value is necessary to the argument or can be bracketed; and whether Marx's method can be separated from his political program.

Important works:

The German Ideology (with Friedrich Engels, 1845–1846).

The Communist Manifesto (1848).

A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859).

Capital, Volume I (1867).

Critique of the Gotha Programme (1875).

Grundrisse (1857–1858).

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831)

Relevant contributions:

Dialectical philosophy.

Historical development.

Contradiction and transformation.

The relationship between individual freedom and social institutions.

What this project takes from Hegel: the idea that historical development proceeds through contradiction and transformation rather than as a smooth accumulation; and the idea that individual freedom and social institutions are mutually constitutive rather than opposed.

What this project leaves open: whether dialectical method can be retained without Hegel's idealism; and whether the dialectical account of history is compatible with a materialist, non-teleological account of social change.

Marx critically adapted Hegel's dialectical philosophy, shifting the emphasis toward material activity and historical social relations. The relationship between Hegelian dialectics and historical materialism deserves careful study rather than reducing it to a simple formula.

Friedrich Engels (1820–1895)

Important works:

The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845).

Anti-Dühring (1878).

Socialism: Utopian and Scientific (1880).

The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884).

Why Engels matters to this project: The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State is the most sustained attempt in the classical Marxist tradition to explain how political institutions emerge from economic organization, and it is therefore directly relevant to the question of what replaces those institutions. The Condition of the Working Class in England is an early model for investigating material conditions empirically rather than abstractly.

3.2 Classical anarchist political economy
Anarchism already contains substantial traditions of economic and social analysis. The project should investigate these rather than assume anarchism lacks an intellectual foundation. What follows is not a survey of anarchist thought as such, but a survey of the thinkers whose economic and organizational arguments bear directly on this project.

Errico Malatesta (1853–1932)

Relevant concepts:

Emma Goldman (1869–1940)

Relevant concepts:

Individual freedom.

Social emancipation.

Criticism of institutional authority.

The relationship between personal autonomy and social organization.

Works:

Anarchism and Other Essays (1910).

My Disillusionment in Russia (1923).

Voltairine de Cleyre (1866–1912)

Relevant concepts:

Freedom of association.

Anarchism without adjectives.

Pluralism between competing anarchist economic arrangements.

Her work is useful for preventing this project from assuming that every aspect of economic organization must have one universally imposed solution.

What these thinkers add up to, and where they disagree. Taken together, the classical anarchist tradition offers a serious economic program: mutual credit and federated exchange (Proudhon), decentralized production and regional self-sufficiency (Kropotkin), and voluntary association as the basis of political organization (Bakunin, Malatesta, Goldman). It also offers a pluralist method: de Cleyre's "anarchism without adjectives" is a standing argument against treating any one of these arrangements as the only legitimate one. Where they disagree is on the boundary between market exchange and common ownership (Proudhon vs. Kropotkin), on the role of organized revolutionary action (Bakunin and Malatesta vs. Goldman's later skepticism), and on whether anarchism requires a single economic model at all (de Cleyre's answer: no). Those disagreements are part of the research subject, not obstacles to it.

3.3 Social ecology and post-scarcity anarchism
Murray Bookchin (1921–2006)

This is one of the most important predecessors to investigate.

Bookchin explored the relationship between technological development, ecological conditions, hierarchy, and decentralized social organization.

Works:

Post-Scarcity Anarchism (1971).

Toward an Ecological Society (1980).

The Ecology of Freedom (1982).

The Philosophy of Social Ecology (1990).

Particularly important:

Post-Scarcity Anarchism examines the possibility that technological development could reduce material scarcity and create the conditions for greater human freedom.

The Ecology of Freedom investigates hierarchy as a social and historical phenomenon.

His later Communalism develops ideas around directly democratic municipalities and confederations.

Bookchin eventually distanced himself from anarchism and developed Communalism as a distinct political framework. That evolution should also be examined.

The central opportunity is to investigate how Bookchin's arguments change when applied to AI, modern automation, digital production, and distributed infrastructure.

5. Commons-based production and the digital economy

Benkler investigates how people can collectively produce valuable resources without relying exclusively on market pricing or managerial hierarchies.

This is directly relevant to the idea that people may create things because they want to, rather than because someone pays them to do so. The project's starting concern about creative work belongs here: people may withhold work not because they oppose sharing, but because their livelihood depends on controlling its release and use. 6. Economic coordination beyond conventional markets and centralized states
A decentralized society still needs to coordinate production, distribution, infrastructure, and resources. This is a major theoretical challenge.

6.1 Elinor Ostrom (1933–2012)
Ostrom investigated how communities govern shared resources without relying exclusively on privatization or centralized government.

Important works:

Governing the Commons (1990).

Understanding Institutional Diversity (2005).

Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems (2009 lecture and 2010 article).

Important concept: polycentric governance. This involves multiple overlapping centers of decision-making rather than one centralized authority.

Her work is particularly valuable because it investigates actual institutions and their conditions for success.

Important questions:

How are shared resources monitored?

How are disagreements resolved?

How do local arrangements coordinate with larger systems?

7. Automation, post-work politics, and post-capitalism
These thinkers agree that automation changes the terms of the work debate. They disagree sharply about whether abundance is arriving, whether it should be demanded, what role the state plays in getting there, and whether the work ethic itself is the problem. The differences matter for this project, because a materialist account of anarchism needs to be specific about what it thinks automation can and cannot do.

7.1 Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams
Work:

Inventing the Future: Postcapitalism and a World Without Work (2015).

Important proposals:

Full automation.

Shorter working weeks.

Universal basic income.

Challenging the conventional work ethic.

Building political capacity for technological transformation.

Where they stand: strategic. Automation is not treated as inevitable; it is treated as something to be demanded and organized for. Their contribution to this project is the insistence that technological capability is a political question, not a technical one.

7.2 Paul Mason
Work:

PostCapitalism: A Guide to Our Future (2015).

Investigates information technology, economic transitions, networked production, and the possibility of post-capitalist economic relationships.

Where he stands: descriptive. Mason argues that information technology is already dissolving capitalist relations from within. His contribution is the argument that the transition is underway, not just possible.

7.3 Aaron Bastani
Work:

Fully Automated Luxury Communism (2019).

Explores technological abundance and the possibility of transforming economic production through automation and technological development.

Where he stands: optimistic-utopian. His contribution is to make the case for abundance as a realistic horizon; his claims should be critically examined rather than accepted as guaranteed technological outcomes.

7.4 André Gorz
Relevant works:

Critique of Economic Reason (1989).

Reclaiming Work (1999).

Gorz investigates the relationship between labor, economic necessity, autonomy, and alternative forms of social organization.

Where he stands: skeptical of the work ethic itself, and less confident than Bastani that abundance is arriving. His contribution is the argument that the problem is not just how much work there is, but how work is organized and what it means.

7.5 Universal basic income and universal basic services
These are related but distinct proposals. Universal basic income provides individuals with an unconditional monetary income. Universal basic services focuses on ensuring access to essential services and resources.

Both deserve examination, particularly in relation to the possibility that technological productivity could reduce the amount of labor required to provide necessities. Neither automatically eliminates capitalist ownership or economic dependency.

8.3 Creative production and the video game example
Consider someone independently creating a video game with AI-assisted tools. Another person might:

Reverse-engineer its mechanics.

Create a compatible implementation.

Modify the game.

Translate it.

Create new assets.

Develop an entirely different experience using the original as inspiration.

Under a conventional commercial model, this can threaten the original creator's revenue. The creator may therefore hesitate to release source files, assets, or details that would make reuse easier—not necessarily because they oppose sharing, but because their income depends on sales, commissions, licensing, or some degree of exclusivity. Fear of having work taken or commercially reproduced without permission can be a rational response to an arrangement in which the creator bears the financial risk.

The research question is not whether creators ought to give everything away. It is what material and institutional conditions would let them choose whether, how, and under what terms to share. If access to necessities were less dependent on selling creative output, the economic stakes of sharing or allowing others to build on a work could change. That would not automatically settle questions of rights, consent, attribution, or fair treatment.

Creators may still care about:

Attribution and recognition.

Consent and the terms of reuse.

Creative control and the ability to set boundaries.

Privacy.

Compensation for work they choose to commercialize.

Their relationships with audiences and communities.

The point is not to make these concerns disappear. It is to investigate whether they can be addressed without making financial survival depend so heavily on restricting access to creative work.

Important distinction (developed further in §9): the ability to create something, the right to access it, the ability to modify it, ownership of it, and the ability to earn a livelihood from it are separate questions. AI may change the first of these dramatically without automatically resolving the others.

9. The central economic distinction: production versus survival
This is potentially one of the most important foundations of the project. It formalizes the distinction introduced in §8.3 and carries it through the rest of the document.

Modern productive systems frequently bundle together several separate concepts:

Producing something useful.

Controlling the resources required to produce it.

Controlling access to the resulting product.

Receiving compensation for the work.

Obtaining the necessities of life.

These relationships are historically contingent rather than necessarily inseparable.

A person can produce something valuable without wanting to sell it. A community can maintain useful infrastructure without treating every contribution as a commercial transaction. A person can participate in creative activity without expecting financial compensation.

The creator's dilemma makes the distinction concrete. If releasing a work exposes it to unauthorized copying or substitution, and the creator relies on that work for income, then control over access can become tied to economic survival. Simply advocating openness does not resolve this conflict. Nor does separating survival from production imply that all work should become freely available or that creators should lose control over how it is used.

The challenge is to understand how access to necessities can become less dependent on selling labor, while preserving meaningful choices around consent, attribution, recognition, privacy, compensation, and creative control. Greater material security could change the bargaining conditions around sharing; it does not predetermine what creators should choose.

This distinction should guide much of the future research.

11. Important contradictions and criticisms
A credible theory must investigate its own weaknesses.

Technological abundance versus material scarcity
Digital goods can often be copied cheaply. Land, energy, physical infrastructure, and ecological resources remain constrained. A society cannot assume that every resource becomes unlimited through automation.

Decentralization versus coordination
Local autonomy is valuable, but large infrastructure systems require coordination across communities. How can this happen without creating permanent centralized authority?

Voluntary contribution versus necessary labor
Some necessary activities may remain undesirable, dangerous, or difficult. How should societies distribute these responsibilities fairly?

Shared resources versus individual autonomy
Commons governance must not become another mechanism for controlling individuals. How can people participate without surrendering personal autonomy?

Automation versus ownership
Automation can reduce labor requirements while concentrating productive wealth in the hands of infrastructure owners. Technological progress alone does not guarantee emancipation.

Open production versus creator recognition
How can communities encourage sharing, remixing, and collaboration while respecting the interests and agency of creators?

Cooperation versus hierarchy
Informal hierarchies can emerge even in organizations that formally reject authority. How can communities recognize and address those dynamics?

13. Preliminary theoretical direction
A possible synthesis, stated as a position rather than a list of sources.

Marx provides a method for investigating the historical development of productive capabilities and the contradictions of existing economic relations. Anarchist traditions provide extensive analysis of authority, mutual aid, voluntary association, federation, and decentralized social organization. Bookchin provides an important connection between technological development, ecology, hierarchy, and post-scarcity possibilities. Ostrom provides empirical research into shared resource governance. Benkler provides an account of decentralized peer production. Cybernetic and computational socialism provide approaches to economic coordination. Contemporary AI research raises new questions about infrastructure ownership, productive capabilities, and technological power.

The direction these sources suggest is a materialist account that takes Marx's historical method without his political program, takes anarchism's commitment to decentralized organization without its tendency to treat economic coordination as an afterthought, and uses Ostrom, Benkler, and the computational planning literature to fill the coordination gap that both traditions leave open. The production-versus-survival distinction (§9) is the analytic hinge: it is what allows the project to separate the question of what can be produced from the question of who controls access to it, and therefore from the question of how people obtain the necessities of life.

Whether that synthesis holds is what the rest of the project needs to test. The next priorities are concrete:

Read Bookchin's Post-Scarcity Anarchism alongside Kropotkin's Fields, Factories and Workshops.

Compare Marx's historical materialism with anarchist critiques of historical development and state power.

Examine Benkler's peer-production framework and Ostrom's commons governance together.

Investigate cybernetic socialism and computational planning, especially their centralization risks.

Map contemporary AI ownership and infrastructure against the possibility of community-controlled production.

Develop a theory of economic dependency that distinguishes productive work from the need to earn a living.

Investigate practical examples and counterexamples rather than relying exclusively on theoretical arguments.

The task is to understand the relationships, disagreements, limitations, and possibilities of these traditions, and then to say something they do not already say on their own.

17. Beyond ideological labels: the future of humanity

The project's scope is broader than the name of any political tradition. Marxism, socialism, communism, anarchism, capitalism, liberalism, and other labels are historically useful: they identify intellectual traditions, movements, institutional arrangements, and disagreements. They should not become substitutes for investigating what helps humanity live, develop, cooperate, and flourish.

The central subject is the future of human civilization. Political and economic traditions are sources of hypotheses, historical experience, concepts, and proposed institutions. None should be treated as the predetermined destination or final answer.

A guiding question:

How can humanity develop its productive, intellectual, technological, social, and ecological capabilities in ways that progressively expand freedom, material security, meaningful choice, and the possibility of human flourishing?

This framing does not assume a single ideal society, a fixed endpoint of history, or one universal way of living. It leaves room for pluralism, experimentation, disagreement, institutional change, and different communities choosing different arrangements, provided those arrangements do not depend on domination or deny others the conditions of freedom.

The working title remains a historical and intellectual point of entry, not a boundary around the investigation.

18. Human flourishing and the capability approach

A society's progress should not be measured only by output, income, technological sophistication, or aggregate growth. It should also be examined in terms of what people are actually able to do and become.

The capability approach, associated especially with Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum, distinguishes resources from the real opportunities people have to use them. Equal resources do not necessarily produce equal freedoms: health, disability, social conditions, public infrastructure, discrimination, care responsibilities, and personal circumstances affect what people can actually do.

Important contributors and works:

Amartya Sen — Development as Freedom (1999). Connects development to substantive freedoms, including political participation, access to education and health, economic opportunity, and the ability to shape one's life.

Martha C. Nussbaum — Creating Capabilities: The Human Development Approach (2011). Develops a philosophical account of central human capabilities and the conditions needed for a life worthy of human dignity.

Manfred Max-Neef, Antonio Elizalde, and Martín Hopenhayn — Human Scale Development: Conception, Application and Further Reflections (1986; English-language editions followed). Distinguishes fundamental human needs from the different satisfiers through which societies attempt to meet them. This is useful for asking whether institutions meet needs in ways that support or undermine autonomy.

Questions for this project:

- What should count as progress if GDP and production volume are insufficient measures?
- Which capabilities should everyone have a genuine opportunity to develop?
- How can a society secure common foundations without prescribing one model of a good life?
- How do power, disability, care, culture, and unequal starting conditions shape people's real freedom?
- How can capability and flourishing be evaluated without reducing them to one score or imposing one definition of happiness?

19. Prosperity, post-growth, and ecological limits

The future of humanity cannot be considered separately from the material and ecological systems that sustain it. The question is not simply how to produce more, but what should be produced, for whom, at what cost, and within which ecological limits.

Post-growth research investigates how societies might prioritize wellbeing, sufficiency, equality, and ecological stability rather than treating perpetual GDP growth as the overriding objective. This is not identical to recession, austerity, or indiscriminate reduction of useful production. It asks how to reduce harmful and unnecessary throughput while improving access to the goods, services, and social conditions people need.

Important contributors and works:

Tim Jackson — Prosperity Without Growth: Foundations for the Economy of Tomorrow (2009; substantially revised second edition, 2017). Examines the meaning of prosperity in a finite world and the institutional dependence of employment, investment, and public provision on economic growth.

Kate Raworth — Doughnut Economics: Seven Ways to Think Like a 21st-Century Economist (2017). Presents a framework with a social foundation below which people should not fall and an ecological ceiling that humanity should not overshoot.

Giorgos Kallis, Jason Hickel, Daniel W. O'Neill, Tim Jackson, Peter A. Victor, Kate Raworth, Juliet B. Schor, Julia K. Steinberger, Diana Ürge-Vorsatz, and colleagues — "Post-growth: the science of wellbeing within planetary boundaries" (The Lancet Planetary Health, 2025). A review of research on wellbeing within planetary boundaries, including ecological macroeconomic models, growth dependencies, and provisioning systems.

Aljoša Slameršak, Vivien Fisch-Romito, Jason Hickel, Jarmo S. Kikstra, Joel Millward-Hopkins, Yannick Oswald, Julia K. Steinberger, and colleagues — "Principles for a post-growth scenario of ambitious mitigation and high human well-being" (Nature Climate Change, 2026). Synthesizes post-growth principles around wellbeing, sufficiency, reduced inequalities, repurposing the economy, and convergence between North and South.

Related research areas:

- Planetary boundaries and Earth-system stability.
- Ecological economics and steady-state economics.
- Degrowth and post-growth political economy.
- Sufficiency, universal basic services, and public provisioning.
- Energy use, material footprints, and decent living standards.
- Care economies and the organization of socially necessary work.

Questions for this project:

- Which forms of production should expand, contract, or be redesigned?
- How can basic security and public services be maintained without depending on endless growth?
- How should ecological costs and resource use be distributed fairly, including between countries and generations?
- What does a materially sufficient but culturally and intellectually expansive society look like?
- How can transition avoid turning ecological limits into an excuse for authoritarian control or unequal sacrifice?

20. Convivial technology and human autonomy

Technology should be evaluated not only by efficiency or novelty, but by the relationships it creates between people, tools, institutions, and infrastructure.

Ivan Illich — Tools for Conviviality (1973) argues for tools and institutions that people can use to pursue their own purposes, rather than systems that turn users into dependent clients of professionalized or centralized institutions. His concept of convivial tools is especially relevant to evaluating AI, software platforms, education, transport, health systems, and communication infrastructure.

Lewis Mumford — Technics and Civilization (1934) examines the historical relationship between technological systems and the development of civilization. His work helps place individual inventions within larger social and institutional transformations.

Murray Bookchin — "Towards a Liberatory Technology" (1965), Post-Scarcity Anarchism (1971), and later social-ecological writing — connects technological design with decentralization, ecological relationships, and the possibility of reducing unnecessary toil.

A useful distinction for this project:

- A tool can increase an individual's capability while increasing their dependence on its owner.
- A system can become more efficient while making its decisions less understandable or contestable.
- Automation can reduce necessary labor while concentrating control over the resulting time and wealth.
- Distributed access does not necessarily mean distributed ownership, governance, or repair capability.

Questions for this project:

- Does a technology expand people's ability to act independently?
- Can people understand, modify, repair, replace, and collectively govern important tools?
- Who controls the infrastructure, standards, data, energy, and interfaces on which a tool depends?
- Does automation distribute free time and capability, or mainly increase the power of owners?
- What should remain small-scale and locally controlled, and what benefits from large-scale shared infrastructure?

21. Systems thinking and civilizational development

Human societies are complex systems. Their behavior emerges from relationships, feedback loops, delays, incentives, material constraints, and institutional rules. Interventions can produce unintended effects, and solutions that work locally may behave differently at larger scales.

Donella H. Meadows, Dennis L. Meadows, Jørgen Randers, and William W. Behrens III — The Limits to Growth (1972). Uses system-dynamics modelling to examine interactions among population, industrial production, resources, food, and pollution over time.

Donella H. Meadows — Thinking in Systems: A Primer (published posthumously in 2008, edited by Diana Wright). Introduces stocks, flows, feedback, delays, resilience, and leverage points. It is useful for analyzing institutions as dynamic systems rather than static organizational charts.

Related work includes systems dynamics, resilience research, cybernetics, Earth-system science, complexity studies, and long-term scenario modelling.

Questions for this project:

- Which feedback loops reproduce poverty, concentration of power, ecological damage, or institutional rigidity?
- Which interventions change the rules and incentives that generate outcomes, rather than treating symptoms?
- How can systems remain resilient without becoming resistant to necessary change?
- How should uncertainty, delayed effects, and unintended consequences influence institutional design?
- How can a society detect failure early and correct course without requiring crisis or collapse?

22. Cooperation, commons, and plural forms of organization

The future of civilization need not depend on choosing between isolated localism and one centralized global authority. A central research problem is how different scales of organization can coexist and coordinate.

This section connects the project's existing research on anarchist federation, mutual aid, commons governance, peer production, participatory planning, and cybernetics with broader questions about human development.

Relevant traditions and research:

- Polycentric governance: multiple, partly autonomous decision-making centers that cooperate, overlap, and constrain one another.
- Commons governance: communities developing rules and practices for shared resources.
- Peer production: distributed contributors producing knowledge, software, and cultural goods through shared infrastructures.
- Federation and confederation: autonomous groups coordinating through delegated, accountable, and revisable arrangements.
- Cooperatives and solidarity economies: shared ownership and democratic control of productive activity.
- Public and universal provisioning: ensuring access to essential goods and services without making every need dependent on individual purchasing power.
- Participatory planning and cybernetics: coordinating complex activities through information, feedback, and accountable decision-making.

Existing thinkers already covered elsewhere in this document—Elinor Ostrom, Yochai Benkler, Peter Kropotkin, Murray Bookchin, Stafford Beer, Michael Albert, Robin Hahnel, and others—should be revisited through this broader civilizational question rather than treated as representatives of sealed ideological camps.

Questions for this project:

- Which decisions should be made individually, locally, regionally, or globally?
- How can coordination remain accountable to the people affected by it?
- How can people leave, challenge, or redesign institutions without losing access to necessities?
- How can plural communities share infrastructure and manage conflicts without requiring cultural uniformity?
- What prevents federations, cooperatives, and commons from developing informal elites or bureaucratic inertia?

23. Culture, meaning, creativity, and chosen activity

Material security is necessary for freedom, but it is not a complete account of human flourishing. People also seek meaning, relationships, play, discovery, identity, belonging, mastery, beauty, and creative expression. These purposes are diverse and cannot be fully specified by economic planning or a single political philosophy.

The project should investigate how social conditions affect the freedom to choose activities, not assume that removing economic compulsion makes all activity effortless or that people will share one account of a meaningful life.

The earlier game-development example belongs here. If people have secure access to necessities and creative tools, they may be freer to make, modify, preserve, share, and experiment without needing every activity to become a revenue source. But attribution, consent, privacy, recognition, control over one's work, community relationships, and the ability to support chosen projects still matter.

Questions for this project:

- What forms of work and effort do people choose when survival is less dependent on selling labor?
- How do creativity, play, education, care, and discovery contribute to flourishing?
- How can communities support shared culture without imposing uniform tastes or values?
- How should rights, attribution, consent, and stewardship work when copying and modification become easier?
- How can people pursue meaningful lives that are not measured by productivity?

24. A broader research framework

The following dimensions can be used to evaluate proposed futures, technologies, and institutions. They are questions for investigation, not a universal scorecard.

- Material security: are the conditions for a decent life reliably available?
- Real capabilities: can people actually use available resources and opportunities?
- Autonomy: can people make meaningful choices and refuse unwanted arrangements?
- Power: who sets rules, controls infrastructure, and can enforce decisions?
- Participation: can affected people understand and influence decisions?
- Pluralism: can different ways of living coexist?
- Creativity and learning: can people explore, experiment, and develop skills?
- Care: how are dependency, maintenance, and socially necessary care recognized and supported?
- Ecology: are human activities compatible with durable Earth-system conditions?
- Resilience: can communities withstand shocks and adapt?
- Correctability: can institutions recognize mistakes and change without catastrophic breakdown?
- Intergenerational responsibility: do present arrangements preserve meaningful possibilities for future generations?

No single metric can adequately represent these dimensions. Quantitative indicators can help, but they should be combined with qualitative research, historical comparison, lived experience, and public deliberation.

25. Research method: compare futures, not labels

The project should compare concrete arrangements and their consequences rather than rank ideological identities in the abstract.

For each proposed institution, technology, or social arrangement, ask:

1. What human need or capability is it intended to support?
2. What material resources and infrastructure does it require?
3. Who owns, maintains, and governs it?
4. Who benefits, who bears costs, and who is excluded?
5. What dependencies or concentrations of power does it create?
6. Can affected people challenge, leave, or revise it?
7. How does it perform under ecological constraints and unexpected shocks?
8. What evidence exists from historical or contemporary examples?
9. What are its failure modes and unintended consequences?
10. What alternative arrangements have been tested?

Keep distinctions between established findings, historical interpretations, normative arguments, proposals, and hypotheses. Do not treat a thought experiment as evidence or a model as a demonstrated outcome.

28. Capability-expanding technologies: REA as a case study

A useful way to test the project's larger questions is to examine existing tools that may make specialized capabilities more accessible. The point is not to celebrate novelty, but to ask whether a working system actually changes who can understand, use, adapt, or maintain technology.

REA (Reverse Engineer Anything) — https://github.com/morluto/rea

REA is an open-source software project that presents an AI-agent workflow for investigating software artifacts and applications. Its repository includes a CLI, an MCP server, modular analysis capabilities, integrations with external analysis tools, documentation, and automated tests. It is therefore more substantial than a prompt or conceptual proposal: there is an implemented software project with distinct components that can be inspected.

Why it matters here:

- It potentially lowers the expertise barrier to investigating complex or opaque software.
- It can help turn specialized analysis workflows into reusable tools that more people can access.
- It may support learning, interoperability, software maintenance, preservation, and informed modification.
- It illustrates how AI can act as a capability multiplier when connected to real tools and evidence, rather than operating only through conversational claims.

Evidence and limitations

The existence of source code, a package, integrations, and tests establishes that REA is an implemented project. It does not establish that every advertised workflow works reliably, that every integration is equally mature, or that the tool is accessible to people without technical setup.

In the repository snapshot examined on 2026-11-16, package-building and several source-test jobs passed, while the inspected CI run also contained failures in Python producer tests and JavaScript test shards. One reported Python error involved a mismatch in the arguments expected by `catalog_receipt()`. This is evidence of active implementation and testing, but not a clean all-tests-passing result for that inspected run. CI results are time- and commit-specific and should be checked again before making claims about the current state.

The project was inspected through its repository, source tree, tests, documentation, disclaimer, release information, and an associated CI run. It was not installed or independently exercised end-to-end as part of this research. Its practical performance, setup burden, and individual analysis workflows therefore remain unverified here. Some capabilities depend on external analysis engines or other tooling, which affects cost, accessibility, and how much of the workflow can be run locally.

The project's own disclaimer and the terms governing any target software matter. This case study concerns legitimate analysis, learning, interoperability, preservation, and authorized testing—not bypassing access controls or using the tool against systems without permission.

Questions raised by the case

- Does the tool let a non-specialist complete tasks they could not previously perform, or mainly make existing expert workflows faster?
- Which functions work with free and open tooling, and which require commercial or difficult-to-install dependencies?
- Can users inspect and understand the evidence behind an agent's conclusions?
- Can the workflow be run locally, modified, audited, and maintained without dependence on a single provider?
- Does easier reverse engineering increase users' autonomy, or does it create new dependence on model services, tool vendors, and opaque agent orchestration?
- What measurable tasks can be used to compare REA with existing manual workflows and other tools?

Research significance

REA is a promising example to investigate under convivial technology, capability expansion, and the digital commons. It is not evidence by itself that technical expertise has been democratized. The important question is whether its implementation, cost, reliability, documentation, and governance make the capability genuinely available—and whether users gain understanding and control rather than merely receiving outputs from another black box.

For future case studies, use the same evidence ladder:

1. Claims: what the project says it can do.
2. Implementation: what source code, integrations, and shipped components exist.
3. Verification: what tests, CI, independent evaluations, and reproducible demonstrations establish.
4. Accessibility: what skills, hardware, services, licenses, and costs are required.
5. Autonomy: whether users can inspect, modify, repair, self-host, and leave the system.
6. Consequences: who benefits, who bears risks, and what new dependencies or power concentrations appear.
